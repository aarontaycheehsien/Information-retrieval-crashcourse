[00:00:17.107]: They can learn different relationships because their training examples reward different things. The targets and negative examples give a retriever an operational model of relevance. To understand its high scores, ask where that lesson came from.

[00:00:33.141]: A clicked result records an action. Clicks can reward popularity and ranking position as well as usefulness. A question paired with an answer passage instead rewards an answer-bearing relationship. Neither source directly observes everything a searcher means by relevance.

[00:00:51.113]: Titles paired with abstracts may mainly reward topical similarity. Human judgements, generated queries and neighbouring passages can supply other training signals. Each has advantages and limitations. Knowing the signal helps you ask which relationship a model was encouraged to preserve.

[00:01:11.549]: The chapter gives SPECTER as a deliberate scholarly example. It trains document-level paper embeddings using the citation graph. Citation-linked relationships shape a space intended to reflect scholarly relatedness.

[00:01:26.819]: That is a different target from finding a direct answer to a question. Relatedness can be useful, but it does not prove that a paper answers this search, supports another paper's conclusion, or meets a review's eligibility criteria. The training relationship needs to fit the use.

[00:01:45.149]: A retriever trained on general web question and answer pairs learns from that literature, genre and style of question. A systematic reviewer, a chemistry researcher, or someone searching another language may need distinctions barely represented in those examples.

[00:02:02.385]: Changing the examples can change what ranks highly even with the same architecture. A strong benchmark result may transfer poorly to another collection or query population. Treat transfer as something to evaluate locally, not as a guarantee supplied by a model name.
