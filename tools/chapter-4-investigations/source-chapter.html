Librarians routinely treat lexical and Boolean as synonyms. The reasoning feels natural: if a system returns records that lack one of my words, it can’t be matching words—so it must be doing something semantic.

Yet a lexical system can return a record that lacks one of the query terms. It may require every term, some terms, or any one matching term, while using term matches to calculate scores. This chapter separates four things that the word “search” usually blurs together: query analysis, execution rules, understanding and transformation. It then uses them to show exactly how far the first two opening puzzles can be taken, and where the interface stops answering. The Semantic Scholar example also requires evidence about the retrieval pipeline, which is examined in Chapter 7.

Key distinction

Lexical search does not have to mean Boolean search#

Lexical retrieval is any retrieval that draws its evidence from words: from how the analysed query terms relate to the indexed document terms. Boolean retrieval lives in this family. So does BM25. Both draw their evidence only from words, but use it differently: Boolean treats words as admission rules, BM25 as weighted evidence.

The arrangement librarians know best combines the two: a Boolean expression decides who is eligible, then a ranker orders the survivors. BM25 can also gather and rank candidates directly from records matching any analysed query term.

Suppose the analysed query is:

apple orange banana

A familiar academic-search interpretation is:

apple AND orange AND banana

Under that model, the implied AND admits only records containing every term, and a ranker orders the survivors. A relevance-ranked result list can look similar under different admission rules. The same vocabulary, the same analysis pipeline and the same inverted index support at least three different rules:

  1. All terms required (implied AND, then ranking). A record missing banana is out before the ranker ever sees it.
  2. Some terms required (minimum match). The system sets a threshold—say, two of three terms—and a record clearing it becomes a candidate.
  3. Any term suffices (any-match ranked retrieval—what Chapter 3 called direct BM25 retrieval). One matching term makes a record a candidate; every further match adds weighted evidence. BM25 commonly runs this way, gathering candidates from the union of the posting lists rather than waiting at an AND gate.11

All three are lexical. Not one of them needs embeddings, neural networks or “semantics”. A record lacking a typed word therefore does not establish semantic matching; What might have happened to a missing query word sets out what to check first.

An admission rule, a ranking function and a top-k output boundary therefore solve different problems: who may compete, how candidates are ordered, and how many leaders are retained. A production system can control all three independently.

Historical example

Early Google: a useful counterexample

Google once described multiword searches as AND queries, but later began silently omitting some terms and was described as behaving like a “soft AND.” Ranked lexical retrieval could still order the remaining matches.12 Google introduced BERT into query understanding and ranking only in 2019, though machine-learning ranking components predate it.13

A bad term: exclusion under AND, distortion under BM25#

Consider this query, where foolish is someone’s weak attempt at a synonym for delulu:

delulu job interview expectations foolish

The same bad term does different damage under different rules.

Under implied AND, foolish is compulsory. Relevant records that never use the word are excluded before ranking begins—and nothing downstream can recover them, because no ranker can rank what it never received.

Under any-match BM25, a record can remain eligible through its other matching terms. The added term foolish can still change the ranking: if it is rare in the collection, its high IDF can increase the scores of records containing it, even though it poorly represents the searcher’s need. The Evidence Lab demonstrates the effect.

Term-frequency saturation cannot identify a weak query term; it only limits the benefit of repeating that term inside a document. BM25 is lexical evidence made less binary, not semantic understanding.

Both systems can handle the query as designed and still return poor results because foolish poorly represents the searcher’s need.

What might have happened to a missing query word#

So a result comes back without one of the words you typed. What happened to the word? Four explanations are consistent with what you can see:

  • It was removed during analysis—discarded as a stop word or punctuation before retrieval began.
  • It was removed or changed during rewriting—an upstream component shortened, expanded or reformulated the query. Unlike routine analysis, this is explicit rewriting, which a later section takes up.
  • It survived, but was optional—nothing required it. Matching it could have improved a score, or it may have matched nothing at all, finding no posting list. Either way, other results survive; under strict AND, a required unmatched term would have emptied the result set.
  • It matched evidence outside the visible excerpt—another indexed field, an expanded term, an older indexed version or off-page evidence. Inspect the searchable fields before concluding that no lexical match occurred.

The visible result is consistent with all four explanations, and several may operate in the same search: a system may discard some terms, require only a subset of the survivors, and rank by weighted matches. This is the scite puzzle in miniature. The scite screenshot alone does not show which explanation applies.

The words sent to retrieval may not be the words you typed#

Evidence register · 1 claim

Primo · Research Assistant. An LLM converts a question to Boolean variations connected with OR.

Drop the assumption that the words reaching the index are the words you typed. Many systems first interpret what you typed, then build a different internal query—one better suited to the index behind it. Like analysis, this explicit rewriting changes what reaches retrieval, but it does so by reformulating the request. The book has met it twice already: as one of the four explanations above, and as the first step of Primo Research Assistant’s pipeline in Chapter 1.

That puts four distinct jobs between your keyboard and the posting lists. Each has now appeared in this book; here they are side by side.

  • Query analysis—the routine plumbing of Chapter 2: tokenisation, normalisation, stemming and lemmatisation, stop-words. It prepares strings for matching. It implies no AI and no understanding of meaning.
  • Query execution rules—this chapter’s dial: are the resulting clauses required, optional, or subject to a minimum match?
  • Query understanding—interpreting the request: spotting a phrase, an entity, a date, an intent. Understanding can inform a decision without altering a single word of the query.
  • Query transformation (query rewriting)—actually changing what retrieval receives: correcting, mapping, expanding, restructuring or splitting what you entered.

Query understanding interprets the request and can leave the query unchanged. Query transformation changes what is sent to retrieval. For example, an LLM can rewrite a question as a Boolean expression that a lexical engine then processes, with BM25 used to rank the matching records.

A contemporary interface may accept a natural-language question, identify its dates or proposed filters, and use a parser or language model to construct a Boolean expression. Natural-language input therefore does not imply a non-lexical engine: the transformed query may still go to Boolean admission and BM25 ranking.

Rewriting is the second of the four explanations for a missing word. Understanding alone does not remove a typed word; it matters only when its interpretation causes one of those changes. Chapter 11 returns to these operations with multi-query, routed and feedback-based retrieval available.

The overview below brings the separate controls together, now that each has been explained.

A search system can therefore be non-Boolean without being non-lexical or semantic.

What Part I settles, and what it hands on#

The first two puzzles from Chapter 1, scite and Google Scholar, can be explained using the distinctions introduced in Part I.

What a result screen can settle

This is the one collapse of this kind you can catch from a result screen, which is why it is settled here rather than held over to Part II. It needs nothing a vendor had to write down—only the recognition that a result can lack a typed term for several reasons, including an admission rule other than strict all-term conjunction.

lexical search = Boolean search
A record can come back missing one of your words while the system is scoring on nothing but words. Boolean retrieval is one form of lexical retrieval; minimum-match rules and BM25 ranking can remain lexical too, and only the all-term conjunction requires every processed term. Any-match eligibility can also be expressed as Boolean OR; the separate ranking function supplies graded scores. See Lexical search does not have to mean Boolean search.

The Google Scholar example separates the reported match count from the number of results a user can view. A reported count is an estimate of matches, and a display limit is a display boundary rather than a scoring decision. The verdict above sets out the general form: admission decides who competes, the ranking function orders them, and the output boundary decides how many are kept—three separate controls, of which an interface reports only the last.

These observations do not establish the products’ retrieval architectures.

The Semantic Scholar puzzle is not of this kind. A product named Semantic Scholar tempts you to read a label as an architecture, and nothing visible on its interface can confirm or refute the reading. We do not yet have the distinctions needed to ask whether retrieval itself was lexical, retrieval itself used dense representations, semantic processing occurred only after retrieval, or several stages were combined—which is precisely why the remaining five of the six distinctions listed at the start of Part I wait for the opening of Part II instead. Two of its instances already matter for what follows. Semantic search does not mean dense retrieval: semantic names a goal, and dense retrieval names one technical route towards it. Vector does not automatically mean dense, learnt or semantic: BM25 scores can be held in sparse, vocabulary-aligned vectors, while contemporary bi-encoders usually produce learnt dense ones.

The remaining problem is vocabulary mismatch: a paper about unrealistic expectations may stay out of reach of a search for delulu. A system can bridge that gap in two places:

  • Change the query, keep the index. Correct, expand or map the query, then match terms as before.
  • Change the representation. Encode queries and documents so that texts expressing the same idea are placed near one another whether or not they share any words, and retrieve by proximity rather than by matching.

The next chapters examine the second route while keeping the first in view.

What this chapter established

  1. Lexical retrieval draws its evidence from analysed terms—what a token becomes after tokenisation, case folding and stemming—reached through posting lists, the records containing each term. Boolean-then-ranking is a common academic-search pattern, but minimum-match and any-match ranking are lexical too.
  2. Analysis, execution rules, query transformation — or a term that simply matched no posting list — can explain a missing typed word without implying dense or semantic retrieval. Understanding matters when its interpretation triggers one of those changes.
  3. Admission, ranking and the top-k output boundary are separate controls: admission forms the candidate set of records that may compete, ranking orders it, and the boundary decides how many are kept.
  4. Part I explains the scite and Google Scholar behaviours — and shows why the interface alone cannot identify the mechanism behind either. The Semantic Scholar observation remains open until semantic capability, dense representation and pipeline stage can be separated.

Non-Boolean does not mean non-lexical. Part II next asks how a learnt representation can connect texts that share no analysed terms, then whether that mechanism is the same thing as semantic search.

What are the three independent controls in a ranked lexical search?

The admission rule decides who may compete, the ranking function orders the candidates, and the top-k output boundary decides how many leaders are retained.

An interface accepts a natural-language question and produces an editable Boolean query. What changed?

Query understanding may have recognised concepts or constraints; query transformation produced the changed Boolean retrieval input. The retriever may still use Boolean admission and lexical ranking over an inverted index.
