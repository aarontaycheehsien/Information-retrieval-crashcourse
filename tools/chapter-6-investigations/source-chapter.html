Chapter 5 ended with a contextual language model and two unanswered retrieval questions: what should one vector represent, and what training makes nearby vectors useful for search? This chapter follows those decisions from model tokens through pooling, separate query and document encoding, similarity, and retrieval-oriented examples.

The delulu example remains a teaching case, not evidence that a pretrained model already understands the query. Its point is to make the desired ranking explicit. Positive pairs and hard negatives provide examples of the distinction we want the model to learn. Training aims to give the passage about unrealistic expectations a higher score for this query than the superficially similar alternative.

Model tokens are not indexed terms#

Classic Word2Vec uses a fixed word vocabulary, so an unseen complete word may map to an unknown token.22 Modern encoders usually divide unfamiliar strings into reusable subwords, characters or bytes. This improves coverage but does not imply understanding: a tokeniser can split a string even when the encoder has learnt little about its use. Appendix B gives the detailed worked example.

The word token is also used in lexical indexing, but the units perform different jobs:

A contextual encoder combines these input units and pools them into one representation for the complete query or passage. The next section explains the training that makes distances between those representations useful for retrieval.

How a contextual encoder becomes a retrieval encoder#

What an embedding captures depends partly on the task used to train it. For retrieval, training should favour the query–document relationships the system needs to recognise.

A dense retriever commonly starts with a pretrained language model. Producing one vector for a whole text requires a pooling step that combines the contextual token representations—commonly by averaging them, or by taking the representation computed at the sequence-opening position. Pooling on its own is not enough. Pretraining gives the model broad linguistic patterns, but a model that predicts masked or next tokens does not automatically arrange its pooled vectors well for search. Two texts can sit close together for reasons that have nothing to do with one answering a search for the other. Retrieval training changes the representation so that labelled relevant texts receive higher similarity scores than competing texts.23

Two influential examples make that transition concrete. Sentence-BERT (SBERT) adapted BERT so that independently encoded sentences could be compared efficiently. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) applied the bi-encoder approach directly to retrieval: a question encoder and a passage encoder are trained so that relevant passages score above competing passages. Both approaches train independently encoded representations so that their similarity scores are useful for the intended task.

A worked training example

Suppose the query is:

Am I being delulu about getting this job?

Take the underlying need to be help me judge whether my expectations are unrealistic. A training dataset might then label this passage as relevant:

How to recognise unrealistic expectations during a job search

It might contrast that passage with an easy negative:

A history of agricultural employment

and a hard negative:

Ten signs that you performed well in your job interview

The hard negative shares the broad topic and several important words, but it does not address whether the searcher’s expectations are unrealistic. Under a different reading of the need—someone gauging their chances from how the interview went—it might well be useful, so this example assigns the label for the stated information need. Training encourages the model to score the labelled positive above both alternatives. Different systems implement this intuition with objectives such as triplet loss or contrastive softmax, and the selection of negative examples materially affects what the model learns.242526

Where the training signal comes from

Where did those labels come from? They might be based on human relevance judgements, clicked results, questions paired with answer passages, titles paired with abstracts, citations, automatically generated queries or neighbouring passages from the same document.

Those sources teach different ideas of relevance. Clicks can reward popularity and ranking position. Question–answer pairs favour answer-bearing passages. Citations may teach scholarly relatedness rather than direct relevance. Titles and abstracts may mainly teach topical similarity.

Training pairs are drawn from a particular literature, genre and style of question, and the model learns from the proxies available there. A retriever trained on web question–answer pairs has learnt what a good answer to a general-knowledge question looks like. That is not the same as what a useful paper looks like to a systematic reviewer, what a relevant record looks like to someone tracing a chemical compound, or what a good match looks like in a language or discipline barely present in the training data. Changing the training examples can change which records the model ranks highly, even with the same architecture.

This can be exploited deliberately. SPECTER trains document-level embeddings of papers on the citation graph, so that papers citing one another are pulled together—a definition of relatedness drawn from scholarly practice rather than from web behaviour.27 However, a model that performs well on the benchmark it was tuned for may transfer poorly to a different collection, query population or retrieval task, which Chapter 13’s discussion of out-of-distribution transfer examines.

Positive and negative labels record the task’s intended distinction; a negative example may still be useful for a different information need.28

What the embedding space ends up preserving

A retrieval embedding therefore preserves the similarities and differences that its starting model, training pairs, negative examples, loss function and optimisation process taught it to preserve. If exact identifiers rarely matter during training, it may blur them. If negatives rarely contain contradictions or specialised senses, it may fail to preserve precisely those distinctions.

The training data and objective give a retrieval model an operational model of relevance: they influence which query–document relationships it scores highly. Clicks, answer pairs, citations and other labels are proxies for a useful result, not direct observations of relevance itself.

What this chapter established

  1. Contextual encoding and retrieval are separate things. A pretrained encoder supplies representations; retrieval training gives the model an operational model of relevance, influencing which query–document relationships receive high similarity scores.
  2. Model tokenisation and index analysis are different processes producing different units. A model token is not an indexed term.

Chapter 7 puts such an encoder to work across a whole collection and shows why semantic search need not mean dense retrieval. Chapter 8 asks what a stored vector represents, how it was produced and which document units were indexed.

Check yourself

One encoder is trained on citation-linked papers; another on question–answer pairs. Why might they rank the same collection differently?

Citation-linked pairs reward scholarly relatedness; question–answer pairs reward answer-bearing matches. The positives and negatives change which relationships receive high scores, even with the same encoder architecture. Test the fit to the intended task rather than treating the model family as a guarantee.

A retriever trained on web question–answer pairs is deployed over a chemistry collection and performs poorly. What should you investigate before attributing the failure to training?

Domain or task mismatch is a plausible hypothesis, not an established cause. Check coverage, searchable fields, query processing and candidate cut-offs, then compare performance across a local set of queries. The scenario identifies the retrieval-training data but does not say whether domain pretraining occurred. Neither training lever alone guarantees a fit to the target collection.

Why is a model token not an indexed term?

They are produced by different processes and do different jobs. An indexed term comes out of analysis and points straight at a posting list. A model token is a vocabulary ID fed into an encoder, combined with its neighbours and then pooled away — it has no posting list and is usually not separately inspectable in the final vector.
